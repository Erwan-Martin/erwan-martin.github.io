PhD thesis

27 April 2026 · Western University

Large-scale clustered attractor network model evokes persistent activity in vitro through novel network clamp method

Erwan Martin · Supervisors: Lyle Muller, Wataru Inoue · Doctor of Philosophy in Neuroscience · CC BY 4.0

Download PDF (7 MB) Western repository

The brain is made of neurons that communicate by sending signals to each other. Their combined activity produces thought and memory. A good example is working memory: the ability to hold information, like a phone number, in mind for a few seconds.

A leading theory says working memory is maintained by circuits of neurons that sustain their own activity after the stimulus disappears. Existing computer models of these circuits don’t fully mimic the brain’s natural activity.

We built a new network model whose architecture more closely mirrors the cortex. To test whether it is plausible, we took the signals received by one neuron in the model and delivered them to a real, living neuron in a lab dish. The neuron responded with electrical activity that closely matched what the model predicted.

Ring attractor models have been instrumental in theorizing the neural basis of working memory, with the seminal work of Compte et al. (2000) demonstrating the role of recurrent excitation and slow NMDA dynamics in sustaining persistent activity. However, these models possess significant limitations: they implement a connectivity profile based on short-range excitation that does not reflect the known topology of cortical microcircuits in the prefrontal cortex. Additionally, the ring model framework cannot fully capture the irregular firing patterns observed in vivo. We hypothesize that because of the scale of this canonical ring model, the synaptic signals operate at a larger amplitude than is biologically plausible and would not evoke a physiological response in a real neuron.

To test this hypothesis, we employed our novel “network clamp” technique, injecting simulated synaptic conductances from the model into biological neurons in vitro. This revealed that the excitation-dominated input from the classical model drives the neuron into a non-physiological, overstimulated state characterized by sustained depolarization block and failure to generate action potentials, thus failing to replicate the model’s predicted output.

To address these limitations, we developed a novel spiking neural network that includes a more biologically realistic, clustered excitatory connectivity at much larger scale. In this clustered model, persistent activity emerges from coordinated increase in AMPA, NMDA, and GABA conductances. Those three conductance signals interact to generate a fluctuation-driven regime, which, when injected into real neurons using network clamp, evoked a physiological spiking activity that aligned closely with the model’s prediction.

The difference between the two models is that while the persistent activity in the ring model is dominated by slow excitation that maintains suprathreshold input, the persistent activity in our model is maintained by input fluctuations that drive spiking while voltage remains below threshold on average, a dynamic that avoids depolarization block in biological neurons.

More broadly, this work introduces our network clamp method to test the biological validity of computational models in living neurons. This approach provides a powerful tool for studying the complex interplay between single neuron properties and emergent network dynamics, establishing a crucial bridge between theoretical and experimental neuroscience.
