# First post


Since the creation of ChatGPT in 2022 LLMs and other generative artificial intelligence systems have made spectacular progress sometimes create the impression that those systems are intelligent and costefficient enough to replace human intelligence. Yet beneath their confidence and apparent competence, today’s systems remain very different from biological brains.

Most modern artificial neural networks are trained using an algorithm called backpropagation. This algorithm works in two phases. During the first phase of training, input information moves through the network from the input layer to the output layer. The system then compares its answer with the expected result from the training data set and calculates an error, the distance between the two response. During the second phase, the error is used to calculate the changes to make to each connections in the network model.

This method has been extraordinarily effective, it has been especially exceptional at remaining effective at large network size. But as those larger and larer models require equally large amount of energy, this  exposes a major differences between artificial and biological intelligence.  backpropagation is slow, computationally expensive and highly dependent on large quantities of data. The network must also operate through distinct phases of propagation and correction, with parameters updated in a carefully synchronised process.
A recent article in [Futura-Sciences](https://www.futura-sciences.com/tech/actualites/intelligence-artificielle-voici-chatgpt-autres-ia-sont-encore-tres-loin-cerveau-humain-135337/) describes those differences well, biological neurons do not appear to wait for a global error signal before adjusting their behaviour. They function simultaneously and locally, with each neuron adapting the connection weights in parallele to normal function continously. The brain therefore seems less like a computer performing a centrally coordinated optimisation procedure and more like a vast collection of interacting agents operating in parallel.

The difference becomes particularly obvious when we compare how humans and machines learn. A young child may see a cat only once or a few times before being able to recognise cats in very different situations. By contrast, an artificial neural network generally requires thousands or millions of examples before it can reliably generalise.

Anthony Zador has argued that this comparison may be misleading because much of an animal’s capacity to learn is already encoded in its biological structure. In his 2019 paper, A Critique of Pure Learning, he proposes that animals are not born with a blank neural network that must discover everything through experience. Evolution has already built a highly structured starting point into the brain. Learning then takes place on top of that inherited architecture.

This idea helps explain why a child can learn so rapidly. The brain is not simply applying a generic learning algorithm to raw data. Its neural organisation, instincts, sensory systems and developmental processes have been shaped by millions of years of evolution. What appears to be learning from a single example may therefore depend on a large amount of prior biological knowledge.

This does not negate the impressive capability of artificial neural networks. But the clear difference between the biological brain and artificial network as they are developed right now raises the debate of wether current generative models are capable of reaching  artificial general intelligence (AGI) and even more artificial super intelligence (ASI)  

Yann LeCun, one of the pioneers of modern deep learning and a recipient of the Turing Award, has repeatedly argued that large language models are unlikely to achieve artificial general intelligence on their own. A language model predicts and generates sequences of symbols, but it does not necessarily possess a grounded understanding of the physical world.

For LeCun, a more capable form of artificial intelligence must develop what researchers call a world model: an internal representation of how objects, events and physical processes behave. Such a system would need to learn from observation, maintain a persistent understanding of its surroundings, make predictions and plan actions.

In 2025, LeCun left Meta and founded Advanced Machine Intelligence Labs, a company focused on developing this type of technology. The project reflects his conviction that the next major advance in AI will not come simply from making language models larger, but from building systems that understand the world beyond language.

The remarkable abilities of LLMs might not be as limitless as certain actors in the sector might postulate. Today’s AI systems are powerful statistical machines, while they might reach limit in how there size improve their performance, it is clear that there are possible optimisation that enable unexpected capabilities. But they remain data-hungry, dependent on specialised training procedures and disconnected from the biological mechanisms that allow animals to learn efficiently from limited experience.
